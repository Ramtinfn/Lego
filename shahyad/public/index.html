A tower the people kept

برجی که مردم آن را نگه داشتند

1966 — A 24-year-old's drawing

When Iran announced a national competition for a monument to stand at the gateway of its capital, the winning drawing came from Hossein Amanat — an architecture graduate just twenty-four years old. His idea was audacious: fold twenty-five centuries of Iranian building into a single silhouette.

Look up through the main vault and you see it: the great elliptical arch of ancient Ctesiphon carrying a pointed Islamic arch above it, the two bridged by geometry drawn from Achaemenid Persepolis — and between them, ribbons of turquoise tile, the colour of every dome from Isfahan to Mashhad.

1971 — Shahyad

Completed in 1971, clad in thousands of hand-cut blocks of white marble from the quarries near Isfahan, it was named Shahyad — "memorial of the kings" — and opened as the ceremonial gate of Tehran. Landing at Mehrabad, it is the first thing a traveller sees: the city introduces itself with this tower.

1979 — Azadi

Then history walked through it. In 1979 the square filled with more than a million people, and the tower was renamed Azadi — "Freedom". It has kept both names ever since, the way a family keeps both a birth name and the name everyone actually uses.

Every era since has gathered beneath it — celebrations, mournings, protests, new year crowds. Governments change; the tower stays. It belongs to no decade and to everyone, which is why Iranians abroad keep its picture on their walls: it is the city itself, distilled to one shape.

Whoever you are, wherever you stand — the tower is where Iran meets itself.

Model IV — The Khaju Bridge

مدل چهارم — پل خواجو

Isfahan, around 1650. Shah Abbas II raised a bridge that is also a weir: the Khaju Bridge dams the Zayandeh-rud into a lake upstream and lets it fall through stepped sluices downstream, while two storeys of arcades carry the road across — and at the centre sits the Shahneshin pavilion, the king's seat over the water. This model is the heart of that bridge: the central pavilion and three river bays to each side, cut at the piers so each end shows a true cross-section — vault, deck, road and galleries — on a display base with the river itself, at about 1:115 scale.

Specifications

  • 1,199 pieces in 82 part-and-colour lots
  • 51.2 × 25.6 × 13.1 cm on a 64 × 32-stud base — about 1:115 scale
  • Display base with river, upstream pool and stepped sluices included
  • Digital 90-page, 139-step instruction book, printable at home — no printed booklet
  • Repeated arch bays with prebuilt pier columns and cutwater noses
  • Every piece brand new
Engineering of the design: every one of the 1,199 parts is identified, the full 139-step build order has been walked through — each piece attaches to the standing structure at its step — and the design software reports one connected model with no stability issues. The model is the bridge's central section, not the whole 133-metre span; the section ends are honest, visible cuts. Carry the finished model on its base with both hands.

Model V — The Hafezieh

مدل پنجم — حافظیه

Shiraz, the city of poets. In the Musalla Gardens lies Hafez, the fourteenth-century master whose divan sits beside the Qur'an in many Iranian homes — a tomb people visit to open his poems and ask them a question. The memorial raised over him in the 1930s is this pavilion: eight slender columns carrying a two-tiered copper dome shaped like a dervish's hat. The model sets it in its garden — stepped plinth, tombstone, cypresses, orange trees and clipped hedges — small enough for a desk, complete enough to recognise at a glance.

Specifications

  • 632 pieces in 78 part-and-colour lots
  • 22.4 × 22.4 cm garden base, 17.4 cm to the finial (28 × 28 studs)
  • Garden with cypresses, orange trees, hedges and flowers included
  • Digital 65-page, 59-step instruction book, printable at home — no printed booklet
  • Delicate build: eight bar-reinforced columns and a dome raised on a hidden turntable
  • Every piece brand new
Engineering of the design: every one of the 632 parts is identified, the 59-step build order has been walked through, and the design software reports one connected structure with no stability issues. This is the most delicate model in the collection — eight slender columns carry the whole dome — so build with patience and handle the finished pavilion only by its base.

Questions

Is this an official LEGO® set?

No. This is an independent fan design. It is not made, sponsored or endorsed by the LEGO Group. The compatible edition uses premium bricks from other brands; the genuine edition is assembled by us from genuine, new LEGO® elements purchased at retail.

Are building instructions included?

The Shahyad Tower and Full Square sets come with a printed, full-colour step-by-step instruction book. Arash the Archer, the Khaju Bridge and the Hafezieh come with digital, print-ready instruction books instead — 185, 90 and 65 pages, printable at home. With every set, the pieces arrive new but not pre-sorted, so set aside a little sorting time before you start — most builders find it part of the ritual.

Why doesn't the square include the trees shown in the pictures?

The tree element is a classic piece that left production in 2013, so supply is limited and inconsistent. Rather than delay or inflate the price of every set, the current edition ships without the eight trees and their trunk bricks. The square is complete and displays beautifully without them, and they can be added later without touching the rest of the build.

Is paying on this site safe?

Yes. Clicking "Pay securely" takes you to Stripe — the payment platform used by millions of businesses worldwide — and your card details go only to Stripe, never to us. You can pay by card, Apple Pay, Klarna or Zip, and Stripe emails you a receipt immediately.

We ship tracked worldwide from Australia. Choose your country in the order panel — the list at checkout shows every country we currently deliver to, across Australia, New Zealand, Asia, Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Americas.
